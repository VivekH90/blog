When are languages not regular

Finite automata are powerful precisely because they are finite. But that same finiteness places fundamental limits on the languages they can recognize.

A language is called regular when it can be recognized by a finite automaton. Equivalently, it can be described by a regular expression or generated by a regular grammar.

The interesting question is therefore not merely how to construct a finite automaton, but how to recognize when constructing one is fundamentally impossible.

A finite automaton has only finitely many states. After reading an arbitrarily long input string, it must therefore reuse states. This finite-memory constraint is the source of many impossibility results in the theory of computation.

To show that a language is not regular, we typically assume that it is regular and then derive a contradiction from the consequences of finite-state computation.
